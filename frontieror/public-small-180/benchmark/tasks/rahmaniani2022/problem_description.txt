# Problem Description

An electricity system planner must jointly determine long-term capacity investments and hourly operational dispatch for a regional power grid over a single planning year. The grid comprises a set of spatial zones connected by transmission lines, and each zone contains clusters of generation or storage resources. Resource types include thermal generators (such as natural gas combined-cycle, natural gas combustion turbine, coal, and nuclear plants), variable renewable energy sources (solar and wind), battery storage, and hydropower. In the released MILP convention, UC resources are installed or retired in physical blocks of `capacity_size_MW`, while non-UC resources use integer 1-MW investment increments; non-UC storage energy similarly uses integer 1-MWh increments. The physical cluster size remains available for operational parameters and existing-capacity bookkeeping, but it is not the non-UC investment block. Certain resource clusters are designated as non-retirable, meaning their retirement count must be zero. The final installed power and energy capacities equal pre-existing capacity plus their explicitly declared increment times net investment. New investment is bounded above and retirements cannot exceed pre-existing capacity. Storage energy capacity must lie between a minimum and a maximum duration ratio times storage power capacity. Transmission expansion uses integer 1-MW increments, and each line's `max_capacity_MW` is the maximum final total capacity after adding new investment to existing capacity.

The planning year is divided into subperiods, each consisting of 168 consecutive hours (one week). Each subperiod carries a weight reflecting how many weeks of the year it represents. The hourly weight for each hour within a subperiod equals the subperiod weight divided by 168. The input data specifies, for every zone and every hour, the net electricity demand in MWh. For each resource cluster and hour, an availability factor between zero and one gives the fraction of installed capacity that can be dispatched; thermal and storage resources typically have availability equal to one, while solar and wind availabilities vary by hour. Each resource cluster has a per-MWh variable operating cost, a per-MW-year fixed operations and maintenance cost, a per-MW-year investment cost, and a CO2 emissions factor in tons per MWh. For storage resources, additional parameters specify charging efficiency, discharging efficiency, self-discharge rate, and per-MWh-year energy investment and fixed energy O&M costs. For hydropower resources, a fixed duration ratio (in MWh per MW) relates reservoir energy capacity to power capacity, and an hourly normalized inflow profile gives the fraction of power capacity arriving as inflow each hour. Demand may be partially curtailed: several consumer segments are defined, each with a maximum allowable fraction of zonal demand that may go unserved and an associated penalty cost per MWh. For each consumer segment, zone, and hour, the amount of unserved energy cannot exceed that segment's maximum allowable fraction of the zonal demand in that hour.

A subset of resources is designated as unit-commitment (UC) resources (typically thermal generators). For each UC resource cluster and hour, the planner must determine how many units are committed (online), how many are starting up, and how many are shutting down, with these quantities allowed to take fractional values rather than being restricted to whole numbers. The number of committed, started-up, and shut-down units at any hour, each scaled by the unit size, cannot exceed the installed power capacity. The generation output of a UC resource in any hour must be at least the number of committed units times the unit size times the resource's minimum output fraction, and at most the number of committed units times the unit size times the hourly availability. The commitment status must satisfy a balance: the change in committed units from one hour to the next equals the number of start-ups minus the number of shutdowns, and this balance also applies with wrap-around between the last and first hours of each subperiod. For resources not subject to unit commitment, generation in each hour cannot exceed the availability factor times the installed power capacity. For non-UC, non-storage, non-hydro resources that have a positive minimum output fraction, generation must be at least that fraction times installed capacity in every hour.

Within each subperiod, storage and hydro resources obey inventory-balance dynamics where the first hour of the subperiod wraps around to follow the last hour. For storage, the state of charge at each hour equals the previous hour's state of charge plus charging (withdrawals times charging efficiency) minus discharging (generation divided by discharging efficiency) minus self-discharge losses (the self-discharge rate times the previous hour's state of charge). The state of charge at the first hour of a subperiod links to the last hour of that same subperiod via the same balance equation. The state of charge cannot exceed the storage energy capacity. Additionally, the energy that would be added by charging cannot exceed the remaining room in the storage (energy capacity minus current state of charge), and the energy to be discharged (generation divided by discharging efficiency) cannot exceed the current state of charge. A storage resource's combined injection and withdrawal in any hour cannot exceed its power capacity, and its withdrawal is bounded by availability times power capacity. For hydropower, the reservoir level at each hour equals the previous hour's level plus inflow (the normalized inflow fraction times power capacity) minus generation minus spillage, again with wrap-around between the last and first hours within each subperiod, and the reservoir level cannot exceed the duration ratio times power capacity. Hydro resources must produce generation plus spillage at least equal to their minimum output fraction times power capacity.

Ramping limits restrict how fast resources change output between consecutive hours. For non-UC resources, the increase in generation from one hour to the next cannot exceed the ramp-up rate (as a fraction per hour) times installed capacity, and the decrease cannot exceed the ramp-down rate times installed capacity, with the same wrap-around between the last and first hours of each subperiod. For UC resources, the ramping limits are tighter and account for start-up and shutdown transitions: the increase in output is limited by the ramp-up rate times the unit size times the number of units that were already committed (excluding those just starting), plus the unit size times the lesser of the availability and the greater of the minimum output fraction and the ramp-up rate, times the number of units starting up, minus the unit size times the minimum output fraction times the number of units shutting down. An analogous expression governs ramp-down. These UC ramping rules also apply with wrap-around between the last and first hours of each subperiod.

Committed UC resources must remain online for at least their minimum up-time and, once shut down, must remain offline for at least their minimum down-time. These rules are enforced within each subperiod with wrap-around: the number of committed units at any hour must be at least as large as the sum of start-ups over the preceding minimum-up-time hours (wrapping around within the subperiod), and the number of committed units plus the sum of shutdowns over the preceding minimum-down-time hours cannot exceed the total number of available units (installed capacity divided by unit size).

Power flow on each transmission line in each hour is bounded in absolute value by the line's installed capacity. In every zone and hour, total generation from local resources minus total storage withdrawals, plus net transmission imports (inflows minus outflows), plus any unserved energy across all consumer segments, must equal the zonal demand.

The problem supports three policy scenarios. In the reference scenario, no policy rules are active. Under a renewable portfolio standard, the total weighted generation from all RPS-qualifying resources across all subperiods, plus a per-subperiod noncompliance slack, must be at least a specified share (such as 70 percent) of total weighted system demand. Under a CO2 cap, the total weighted CO2 emissions from all generation and storage withdrawals across all subperiods, minus a per-subperiod noncompliance slack, must not exceed a cap set at 0.05 tons per MWh times total weighted system demand.

The goal is to minimize total annualized system cost. This cost is the sum of six components: fixed costs, comprising investment costs for new generation capacity (unit size times number of new units times per-MW-year investment cost, summed over all resources), plus storage energy investment costs (storage energy unit size times number of new storage energy units times per-MWh-year energy investment cost, summed over all storage resources), plus hydro energy investment costs (per-MWh-year energy investment cost times the hydro duration ratio times number of new power units, summed over all hydro resources), plus transmission line investment costs (per-MW-year transmission investment cost times new transmission capacity in MW, summed over all lines), plus fixed O&M costs on total installed power capacity (per-MW-year fixed O&M rate times installed power capacity, summed over all resources), plus fixed energy O&M costs on total installed storage energy capacity (per-MWh-year energy fixed O&M rate times installed energy capacity, summed over all storage resources), plus hydro fixed energy O&M costs (per-MWh-year energy fixed O&M rate times duration ratio times installed power capacity, summed over all hydro resources); variable operating costs, comprising the hourly-weighted variable cost times generation for each resource and the hourly-weighted variable cost times withdrawals for each storage resource, summed across all subperiods; unserved energy costs, comprising the hourly-weighted penalty cost times curtailed energy for each consumer segment, zone, and hour; start-up costs, comprising the hourly-weighted start-up cost times the number of units started for each UC resource and hour; and policy noncompliance costs, comprising the per-MWh RPS noncompliance penalty times the RPS slack and the per-ton CO2 noncompliance penalty times the CO2 slack, each summed over subperiods.
