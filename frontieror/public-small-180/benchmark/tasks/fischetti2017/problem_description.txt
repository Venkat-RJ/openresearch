# Problem Description

A company must decide which facilities to open from a set of potential locations and how to assign each customer to an open facility, with the goal of minimizing total cost. There are n potential facility locations and m customers. Each facility has a known nonnegative opening cost, and for every facility-customer pair there is a known nonnegative allocation cost that reflects the cost of serving that customer from that facility. In typical applications, allocation costs depend on the distance between the customer and the facility, computed from their geographic coordinates in a two-dimensional space; if a particular facility cannot serve a particular customer, the corresponding allocation cost is treated as prohibitively large.

Every customer must be assigned to exactly one open facility. A customer may only be assigned to a facility that has been opened. There is no limit on how many customers a single facility can serve, which is why the problem is called uncapacitated. The total cost to minimize is the sum of the opening costs of all opened facilities plus the sum of the allocation costs incurred by the customer assignments.

The released benchmark evaluates the separable convex quadratic variant (qUFL): allocation costs are proportional to the square of the fraction of a customer's demand served by a facility, rather than being linear. All allocation cost coefficients are strictly positive, and each facility-customer pair has an associated cost coefficient. Each customer's total demand must still be fully covered, meaning the fractions of that customer's demand served across all facilities must sum to one. A customer can only receive service from a facility that is open, and the fraction of demand served by any facility cannot exceed the open-or-closed status of that facility. The cost incurred for serving a fraction of customer demand from a given facility equals the cost coefficient for that pair multiplied by the square of the fraction served. The goal is to minimize the sum of facility opening costs plus the sum of these quadratic allocation costs across all facility-customer pairs. The stored coefficients can also be used in separate linear-UFL experiments, but linear UFL is not an accepted solution variant for this benchmark case. This quadratic structure arises naturally in applications such as electric power distribution network design, where facilities represent distribution transformers placed near customer premises and the quadratic cost captures power loss in cables, which is proportional to the square of the current flowing through them multiplied by the cable resistance.

The instance data specifies the number of potential facility locations and the number of customers, the two-dimensional coordinates of each facility and each customer, the opening cost for each facility, and the allocation cost coefficient for every facility-customer pair.
