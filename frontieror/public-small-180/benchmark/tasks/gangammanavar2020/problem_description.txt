# Problem Description

A retail distribution network consists of N retailer locations, each identified by its two-dimensional coordinates. Before customer demands are observed, a central planner must choose an order-up-to stock level for every retailer, representing the quantity of inventory to position at that site. Each order-up-to level must be zero or greater. These stock-positioning decisions carry no direct procurement cost; the entire cost arises from the operational actions taken after uncertainty is resolved.

The uncertain elements are customer demands and, for selected retailer pairs, transshipment costs. Demands are governed by a discrete distribution: a finite number of equally structured demand scenarios is given, each specifying a demand realization at every retailer, together with the probability of that scenario occurring. Transshipment cost uncertainty applies only to a designated subset of directed retailer pairs; for those pairs the per-unit shipping cost in a given scenario is drawn from a normal distribution whose mean equals the nominal transshipment cost and whose standard deviation equals a specified fraction of that mean. All remaining retailer-pair transshipment costs are deterministic and equal to their stated mean values. Demand randomness and cost randomness are independent of each other. The input data therefore comprises, for each retailer, a per-unit holding cost, a per-unit penalty (backlog) cost, and mean demand; for each ordered pair of distinct retailers, a mean transshipment cost and, where applicable, a standard deviation; and the full set of demand scenarios with their probabilities, plus a random seed used to generate cost realizations.

Once a scenario is revealed, the planner observes each retailer's actual demand and the realized transshipment costs, and must then decide how to allocate the pre-positioned stock. Specifically, for every retailer the planner determines four quantities: the amount of on-hand stock used directly to serve local demand, the ending inventory remaining after local use and outbound shipments, a replenishment quantity representing the additional stock needed to restore inventory to the order-up-to level after accounting for ending inventory, and a backlog quantity capturing any demand that goes unmet. In addition, for every ordered pair of distinct retailers the planner sets a transshipment quantity, representing units shipped from one retailer to another to help cover the recipient's demand. All of these quantities must be zero or greater.

These operational quantities are linked by four families of balance rules. First, for each retailer the stock used locally plus the total quantity shipped out to all other retailers plus the ending inventory must equal the order-up-to level chosen before uncertainty was revealed. Second, for each retailer the stock used locally plus the total quantity received via transshipment from all other retailers plus the backlog must equal that retailer's realized demand. Third, for each retailer the ending inventory plus the replenishment quantity must equal the order-up-to level. Fourth, across the entire system the sum of all retailers' backlogs and replenishment quantities must equal the sum of all retailers' realized demands.

The goal is to choose the order-up-to stock levels so as to minimize the expected scenario cost, where the cost in any given scenario is the sum of three components: holding costs, computed as each retailer's per-unit holding cost times its ending inventory; penalty costs, computed as each retailer's per-unit penalty cost times its backlog; and transshipment costs, computed as each directed pair's realized per-unit transshipment cost times the quantity shipped along that pair. The expectation is taken over all scenarios, weighted by their probabilities.
