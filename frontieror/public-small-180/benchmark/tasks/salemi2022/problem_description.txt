# Problem Description

A power system operator manages a fleet of thermal generators over a discrete planning horizon consisting of a given number of time periods under demand uncertainty represented by a finite set of scenarios. Each generator has a fixed operating cost paid in every period the unit is running, a per-unit production cost applied to the power it generates, and a start-up cost that depends on how long the generator has been consecutively offline before restarting. The start-up cost is determined by a precomputed schedule that gives the cost of starting a generator after any given number of consecutive inactive periods; this schedule is nondecreasing, reflecting the physical principle that a unit that has been idle longer requires more energy to restart. Each generator also has a minimum and maximum power output when it is running, a minimum number of consecutive periods it must remain on once started, and a minimum number of consecutive periods it must remain off once shut down. Four ramping parameters govern how quickly each generator can change its output between consecutive periods: a ramp-up rate limiting the increase in production while the unit stays on, a start-up ramp rate limiting production in the period a unit first turns on, a ramp-down rate limiting the decrease in production while the unit stays on, and a shut-down ramp rate limiting production in the last period before a unit turns off. It is assumed that each generator's start-up ramp rate does not exceed its ramp-up rate, and its shut-down ramp rate does not exceed its ramp-down rate. All generators are assumed to be offline at the start of the planning horizon.

The operator must make two kinds of decisions. First, for each generator and each time period, the operator decides whether to have the unit running, whether the unit is starting up, and whether it is shutting down; these on/off, start-up, and shut-down choices are made before uncertainty is revealed and must be the same across all scenarios. Second, given a particular demand scenario, the operator decides how much power each running generator produces and how much capacity each running generator makes available in each period; these production and availability decisions are made separately for each scenario.

The start-up, shut-down, and commitment status of each generator are linked by the requirement that the difference between the start-up indicator and the shut-down indicator in any period equals the change in commitment status from the previous period to the current one. Once a generator starts up, it must remain on for at least as many consecutive periods as its minimum up-time requires; specifically, the total number of start-ups occurring within any window of consecutive periods equal in length to the minimum up-time and ending at a period where the unit is considered may not exceed the commitment status at that period. Symmetrically, once a generator shuts down, it must remain off for at least as many consecutive periods as its minimum down-time requires; the total number of shut-downs within any window equal in length to the minimum down-time and ending at a given period may not exceed one minus the commitment status at that period.

The start-up cost incurred by a generator in a given period is at least as large as the precomputed start-up cost corresponding to a certain number of consecutive inactive periods, multiplied by the quantity obtained by subtracting from the current-period commitment status the sum of the commitment statuses over that same number of preceding periods, and this must hold for every such count of consecutive inactive periods from one up to the number of periods that have elapsed before the current one. When a generator is off, its production and available capacity are both zero. When a generator is on, its production is at least its minimum output and at most its available capacity, which is itself at most the generator's maximum output. The increase in a generator's production from one period to the next may not exceed the ramp-up rate multiplied by the previous-period commitment status, plus the start-up ramp rate multiplied by the current-period start-up indicator. The decrease in a generator's production from one period to the next may not exceed the ramp-down rate multiplied by the current-period commitment status, plus the shut-down ramp rate multiplied by the current-period shut-down indicator.

In each scenario and each time period, the total production across all generators must meet or exceed the scenario's demand for that period. Additionally, the total available capacity across all generators must meet or exceed the sum of the scenario's demand and its spinning reserve requirement for that period.

The goal is to minimize the total expected cost, computed as the sum of all generators' fixed operating costs and start-up costs over all periods (these are scenario-independent) plus the average across all scenarios of the total production costs for all generators over all periods.
