# Problem Description

A region is served by an air transportation network in which several nodes represent cities that are simultaneously origins, destinations, and candidate hub locations. One or more incumbent airlines already operate hub-and-spoke networks in this region, each with a fixed set of hub airports. A new entrant airline wishes to enter this market by opening exactly a prescribed number of hubs, chosen from the full set of nodes, so as to capture as large a share of total passenger traffic as possible.

The input data consists of the following. There is a set of nodes, each with spatial coordinates and a fixed waiting time representing the dwell or layover delay passengers experience at that airport. A demand matrix gives the known, non-elastic number of passengers wishing to travel from every origin to every destination. A distance matrix gives the pairwise distances between all nodes. A set of nodes is designated as the fixed hub locations of the competing airlines, and the number of hubs the entrant must open is given. Several global parameters govern customer behavior: an interhub fare discount factor that reduces the fare charged on the hub-to-hub leg of a trip to reflect economies of scale; a time-versus-cost weight that ranges from zero to one and controls how much customers value travel time relative to fare; an attraction decay exponent for travel time; an attraction decay exponent for fare; and a cost scalar that converts distances into fares. In addition, each combination of hub pair and airline has a given attractiveness value that reflects the quality of the airline, the convenience of the hub airports, and whether the route passes through a single hub or two distinct hubs.

Every trip from an origin to a destination may be routed through a pair of hubs, where the two hubs may be the same node (a single-hub route) or different nodes (a two-hub route). For each airline and each ordered hub pair, the total travel time on a route equals the sum of the travel time from the origin to the first hub, the travel time between the two hubs, the travel time from the second hub to the destination, and the fixed waiting times at the origin airport, the first hub, and the second hub. Travel times on each leg are proportional to the distance between the endpoints. The total fare on a route equals the fare from the origin to the first hub, plus the interhub fare discount factor times the fare between the two hubs, plus the fare from the second hub to the destination, where each leg fare is proportional to the distance of that leg scaled by the cost scalar. For each airline, each ordered origin-destination pair (with the origin i and destination j held fixed), and each unordered pair of two distinct hubs {k, l}, only one of the two corresponding two-hub routes is considered open: namely, whichever of the route i -> k -> l -> j and the route i -> l -> k -> j yields the higher customer utility. The origin and destination are never swapped; only the visiting order of the two intermediate hubs is reversed (so the comparison is between two four-node paths that share the same first node i and the same last node j and differ only in the relative order of the two hub nodes in between).

The utility that customers perceive for a particular route operated by a particular airline is the attractiveness of that hub pair divided by the sum of two terms: the time-versus-cost weight times the total travel time raised to the time decay exponent, plus the quantity (one minus the time-versus-cost weight) multiplied by (the total fare raised to the fare decay exponent). In particular, the exponentiation by the fare decay exponent applies only to the total fare and not to the product of the weight complement and the fare, and the "one minus" applies only to the time-versus-cost weight and not to the entire product; equivalently, denoting the time-versus-cost weight by gamma, the total time by t, the total fare by c, the time decay exponent by beta, and the fare decay exponent by lambda, the denominator equals gamma * t^beta + (1 - gamma) * c^lambda. The probability that a passenger traveling from a given origin to a given destination chooses a particular route offered by the entrant airline equals the utility of that route divided by the sum of the utilities of all routes offered by all airlines, including all routes the entrant offers through its opened hub pairs and all routes every incumbent airline offers through its fixed hub pairs. The expected number of passengers captured by the entrant on a given route is the demand for that origin-destination pair multiplied by this probability.

The entrant must select exactly the prescribed number of nodes at which to open hubs. A hub pair is considered active for the entrant only if both nodes in the pair have been selected as hubs. The goal is to maximize the total expected number of passengers captured by the entrant across all origin-destination pairs and all of the entrant's active hub-pair routes, where capture on each origin-destination pair is the demand times the fraction of total utility contributed by the entrant's active routes.
